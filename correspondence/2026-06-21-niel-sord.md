Hi, Niel

Your option (2) is the right instinct, and here's how to make it principled. The terminology has two jobs, and they come apart.

In the running text, keeping the English term (noun, verb, noun phrase) is usually the clearer choice, for exactly the reason you give: the book is about English, the examples are English, and a Filipino reader shouldn't have to relearn pangngalan and pandiwa to follow an argument about English. No objection from me.

The glossary is where the care goes. For each load-bearing term, pin down the test that tells you when it applies. Determinative is defined by what the word does and where it sits, not by what it means. A bilingual glossary that fixes each term by its test lets you keep the English term in the body with a clear conscience.

The one thing to watch is the quiet pull of the traditional Tagalog school-grammar terms. Where one looks like a translation but draws a line in a different place (category and function are the usual casualties), flag it rather than adopt it. That's the analytic work, and it's why a fluent machine pass can't do it for you.

So: English terms in the text, a bilingual glossary that defines by test, and a short watch-list of traditional terms to handle with care. Does that match how Chapter 1 is feeling?
